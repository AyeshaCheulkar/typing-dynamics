01

Research question

Does writing about a happy or a sad moment show up in typing behaviour, focus and mind-space — and do transparent formulas track what people report?

Participants write about a happy or a sad moment, then rate mood, focus, stress and effort. The indices are relative proxies of typing behaviour, not clinical measures.

Headline test — emotional writing vs the person's own baseline

H1: rhythm variability (CV of the inter-key interval) is lower when writing about an emotional moment than in the same person's neutral baseline passage.

{% if live.replication.n_pairs == 0 %}

Why this test: a personal-baseline classifier did not improve happy-vs-sad accuracy on EmoSurv, but a paired within-person comparison revealed one effect that survives length matching. We derived H1 from EmoSurv and now test it prospectively on our own participants (each writes a length-matched neutral baseline first).

06

Literature & caution

  • Epp, Lippold & Mandryk (2011) — emotional states from keystroke dynamics.
  • Vizer, Zhou & Sears (2009) — stress detection from keystroke and linguistic features.
  • BiAffect studies (e.g. JMIR 2024) — typing variability, speed and accuracy linked to mood.
  • Maalej & Kallel — EmoSurv keystroke-emotion dataset (IEEE DataPort).
  • Hutto & Gilbert (2014) — VADER; Rude, Gortner & Pennebaker (2004) — first-person pronouns in emotional writing.

Small sample, self-selected recall, no diagnostic claim. Differences between emotions in external data are group-level; they do not let us label an individual's feelings.
